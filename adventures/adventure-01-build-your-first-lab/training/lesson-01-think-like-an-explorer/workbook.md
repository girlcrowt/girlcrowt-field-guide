## Purpose

This workbook establishes my starting point as an explorer of the world of IT.

As I complete future Adventures, I will revisit this workbook periodically. Updating my baseline is part of documenting my growth as an engineer.

## Section 1 · Starting Point

### How did technology become part of my life?

I think the biggest turning point was the internet.

It completely changed the way I saw the world.

All of a sudden there was knowledge everywhere. For free. Growing up in a small town  (with an even smaller library) that blew my mind. 

I had the absolute privilege of growing up alongside the internet. We were both figuring things out at the same time. Instead of exhausting my parents with endless rabbit hole questions I could now simply go find the answers myself.

When I started working in television, that curiosity stayed with me. My job focused on content, but I was always interested in the technical side as well. I wanted to understand what the equipment could do, how the workflows worked and what was technically possible. That made me better at planning creative projects because I understood both the editorial and the technical perspective.

Years later, during a professional dry spell I took classes on artificial intelligence. Why? Well, to be honest: AI had become such a hot topic in television that learning about it felt inevitable. And I didn’t want to fall behind. Those classes included a class on basic Python. 

Programming surprised me.

I expected it to be interesting, but ultimately a tool to understand AI better. Then I fell in love. 

For the first time, I realized I could combine problem solving, storytelling and creativity in a completely different way. Building software felt strangely familiar.

That's when I started thinking that maybe technology wasn't just another interest.

Maybe it was the natural next step in my career. 

So, when the necessity of professional retraining became imminent, I already knew where I wanted to go:

Hello IT!

I honestly expected to struggle just to keep up because it was so very different from what I’ve been doing those past few decades. Instead, I discovered that I genuinely love learning this stuff. Much more than I ever expected.

And not be dramatic (says the tv producer) but looking back now, I don't think technology suddenly became part of my life.

It had quietly been there all along.

And the more I learned, the more I realized that no part of technology exists on its own. Everything connects to something else. 

And I’m nothing if not a sucker for symbiotic systems.

### When I get stuck, my first instinct is to...

When I get stuck, my first instinct is to go back to the last point where things still made sense and work forward from there. 

If that doesn't work, I start at the very end of the system and peel away the layers one by one until I understand how everything fits together. 

Maybe that's something I brought with me from television. 

You don't just point a camera at people and hope for the best. You first need to know what you're trying to make. 

Once I know where I'm trying to end up, figuring out the steps in between becomes a lot easier. I guess I learn technology the same way. I like to see the whole picture first and then work my way toward the details.

### Which kinds of explanations help me most?

No single explanation works for every topic, but over time I've noticed a few recurring patterns. These are the approaches that consistently help me understand, remember and apply new concepts.

| Learning Preference | Why it works for me |
|---------------------|---------------------|
| **Start with the big picture** | I understand new topics much faster when I first know what they're trying to accomplish and how they fit into the larger system. Once I see the overall structure, the details have somewhere to belong. |
| **Show relationships** | I understand concepts more easily when I can see how they interact with each other. Tables, diagrams, comparisons and visualizing the connections between different technologies help me build a coherent mental model. |
| **Structural analogies** | I learn best when new concepts can be connected to systems I already understand. Recognizing similar structures across different fields helps me build mental models and transfer knowledge between disciplines. |
| **Explain the *why* before the *how*** | Once I understand *why* something exists, what problem it solves and why it was designed that way, remembering *how* it works usually comes naturally. |
| **Layered explanations** | I prefer explanations that gradually move from the overall concept to the individual components and finally to the implementation details. |
| **Build before memorizing** | I learn far more by creating, experimenting and fixing mistakes than by reading or memorizing theory alone. Building projects helps concepts stick. |
| **Questions instead of immediate answers** | When appropriate, I prefer being guided toward an answer instead of being given the complete solution immediately. Working through the logic myself leads to a much deeper understanding. |
| **Room to explore** | I like asking "why?" and following rabbit holes. Exploring related concepts often deepens my understanding of the original topic. |
| **Honest complexity** | I don't want difficult concepts oversimplified or hidden. I'd rather understand the real thing one step at a time than learn an inaccurate shortcut. |

### Which situations make learning challenging for me?


| Situation | Why it makes learning challenging |
|-----------|---------------------------------|
| **Learning without context** | I find it difficult to stay engaged when I don't yet understand why a concept exists or what problem it's trying to solve. |
| **Passive memorization** | Memorizing isolated facts without understanding the underlying concepts rarely sticks. I learn much better by understanding relationships and applying knowledge. |
| **Information without context or application** | I enjoy learning large amounts of information, but I struggle with information dumps that aren't connected to a bigger picture or followed by practical application. I learn best when new knowledge has a clear purpose and can be put to use. |
| **No opportunity to experiment** | I enjoy trying things myself and it helps me deepen understanding. When I can't explore, build or make mistakes, my understanding tends to stay superficial. |
| **Repetition without purpose** | Repeating the same task without learning something new or understanding why I'm doing it makes it difficult for me to stay engaged. |
| **Oversimplified explanations** | Explanations that leave out too much detail or gloss over important concepts or relationships often create more frustration than clarity. |
| **Lack of curiosity** | When a topic doesn't spark my curiosity or challenge me to think, I find it much harder to stay mentally engaged. I naturally learn best when I'm actively exploring and asking questions. |
| **Learning without feedback** | I learn best when I can test my understanding and adjust my thinking. Without feedback, it's difficult to know whether I'm building the right mental model. |

## Section 3 · My Engineering Habits

### When I encounter an error, I usually...

When I encounter an error, my first instinct is to figure it out myself. I usually start by reading the error message (a few times), comparing it to what result I expected and working backwards until I find the point where things stopped making sense. If I ask for help, it's usually because I've reached the edge of my current understanding and need a fresh perspective, not because I want the answer handed to me. That'd be boring. And I enjoy tinkering. 

However, if on a deadline I'll adapt and use all resources available to me asap. "The show must go on!" clearly tumpf "My ego wants to figure this out all by herself!". ;)

### Three skills I most want to develop

**1. Systems Thinking**

   I want to understand how individual technologies work together as part of a larger system instead of learning them in isolation. The connections between things are often just as important as the things themselves.

**2. Problem Solving**

   I want to become the kind of person who approaches unfamiliar problems with curiosity instead of hesitation. My goal isn't to know every answer, but to develop the experience and confidence to find them.

**3. Documentation & Knowledge Sharing**

   I want to turn what I learn into something useful for both Future Me and anyone else who might be walking a similar path. Good documentation isn't just a record of what worked—it's a way of making knowledge easier to build upon.
